Reasoning Under Uncertainty

Reasoning Under Uncertainty GATE DA 📖 Topic Overview

In the real world, AI agents rarely have complete, perfect knowledge. Sensors are noisy, information is incomplete, and the future is unpredictable. Reasoning Under Uncertainty provides a principled framework — based on probability theory — to handle this uncertainty and still make rational, optimal decisions.

Why Not Just Use Logic? Logic requires statements to be definitely True or False. But "the patient has disease X" is not definitely true — it has a probability based on symptoms, test results, and medical history. Probability theory handles this naturally where logic cannot.

The Problem of Uncertainty

Real-world AI agents face uncertainty from multiple sources:

SourceExample
Partial Observability A robot cannot see behind walls
Noisy Sensors A temperature sensor has measurement error
Stochastic Actions A robot arm may slip when picking up objects
Incomplete Knowledge A medical AI doesn't know all possible diseases
Adversarial Environments Opponent in a game makes unpredictable moves
Solution: Instead of a binary True/False belief, an AI agent maintains a probability distribution over possible states of the world. As new evidence arrives, the distribution is updated using Bayes' Theorem.

Probability as the Foundation

The Bayesian approach to AI treats probability as a degree of belief. All uncertainty is represented as probability and updated consistently as new evidence is observed.

Key Insight

  Agent's belief about world = Probability distribution P(X₁, X₂, ..., Xₙ)

  When evidence e is observed:
  Updated belief = P(X | e) via Bayes' Theorem

  Decision = argmax P(outcome | action, evidence)

The Full Joint Distribution

The complete specification of a probabilistic domain is the full joint distribution — a probability for every possible combination of variable values.

For n binary variables → 2n entries needed. For n=30 → over 1 billion entries. This is why we need compact representations like Bayesian Networks.

GATE Tips

  • This entire section builds on top of each other — understand Probability Basics first.
  • Bayesian Networks are the central data structure — understand their structure and CPTs well.
  • Bayes' Theorem is used in almost every question in this section.
  • Conditional Independence is what makes Bayesian Networks efficient — 2n → manageable.
  • Variable Elimination is the exact inference algorithm — know how to apply it step by step.
  • Sampling methods are approximate — Likelihood Weighting is the most efficient of the four.
  • GATE questions typically ask: compute a posterior probability given evidence in a Bayesian Network.
